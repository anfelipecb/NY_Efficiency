# Knowledge card: Executive Order 12, Agency Chief Savings Officers

**In one line:** signed January 29, 2026, it puts a senior Chief Savings Officer (CSO) in every agency to find **recurring, structural** savings, reporting to the First Deputy Mayor and OMB.

## What the order requires

- **Designation:** every agency head names a CSO within 5 days (by February 3, 2026). The CSO reports directly to the agency head, sits on the budget and finance leadership team, and has access to staff and data.
- **Reporting line:** CSOs engage regularly with the **Office of the First Deputy Mayor** and the **Mayor's Office of Management and Budget (OMB)**.
- **Four savings levers** (Section 2). Savings must reduce the agency's baseline budget, not be one-time:
  1. More efficient delivery of services
  2. Consolidating programs
  3. Insourcing work done by outside vendors
  4. Eliminating or sunsetting programs
- **Assessment within 45 days** (Section 3): examine the agency's **five most costly expense-funded programs** to understand cost drivers, evaluate existing initiatives to prioritize those with the strongest results, and review programs that could run more efficiently, including programs created in the past year.
- **Updates every six months** on progress and new opportunities.

## Timeline and results so far

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| Jan 29, 2026 | EO 12 signed. Targets: 1.5% savings in FY2026, 2.5% in FY2027 |
| Mar 20, 2026 | Agencies submit plans identifying more than $1.7B |
| Late March 2026 | City Hall publishes first approved items (examples below) |
| May 2026 | Executive Budget: **$1.77B saved over two years** |
| July 2026 | New round: another **2.5% in FY2027 and FY2028**, starting with a Workforce Savings and Efficiency Survey where employees suggest cuts. Results go into the **November Plan**. City Hall says layoffs are not on the table |
| November 2026 | November Financial Plan, expected to show the second round |

As of October 27, 2026, I did not find a published six-month CSO progress update.

## Where the $1.77B came from (Executive Budget summary)

| Category | Amount (FY26 + FY27) |
|---|---|
| Better financial management, revenue owed to the city, more accurate expense estimates | $947M |
| More efficient public services, managing overtime, phasing out unused programs | $368M |
| Reducing vacancies | $307M |
| Better contract rates, terminating contracts city workers can do better | $94M |
| Technology and software licenses | $28M |
| Giving up unused space, consolidating leases | $24M |

**Analyst's note:** over half of the total is financial management and re-estimates, not changes in how services are delivered. The operational slice ($368M) is the part EO 12's language emphasizes. That is a fair, non-hostile question to ask: how much of the next round will be operational?

## Examples of approved items (March 2026)

- **DOC:** cancel or restructure IT and consultant contracts, bring services in house ($4.3M, FY26)
- **H+H:** contract rebates, less reliance on temporary staffing agencies, **cut overtime**, better revenue collection ($14.1M FY26, $25.7M FY27)
- **FDNY:** bill Medicaid and insurers for "Treat No Transport" ($10.1M FY27)
- **OLR:** audit dependent eligibility in employee health plans (about $100M FY27)
- **DSS:** insource about two dozen IT contracts; end a McKinsey contract (nearly $9M spent in FY26)
- **NYCPS:** end underutilized contracts and cap spending ($30.3M FY27), central office spending controls ($27.5M FY26)

## How it connects to this project

- Pane A (overtime growing faster than the budget) is exactly the "cost driver" analysis EO 12 asks CSOs to do on their five most costly programs.
- Pane B (cost up, outcomes flat or down) is a public-data version of "evaluate existing initiatives to prioritize those delivering the strongest results."
- The mayor framed EO 12 as "savings and efficiencies" rather than austerity. The Reach pane fits that framing: the best savings are ones that do not reduce what reaches residents.

## Interview angles

- "EO 12 asks a good question, which program costs the most and is it working, but each CSO answers it alone. A shared method and a shared scoreboard across 45 agencies would make the six-month updates comparable."
- "Overtime is the cleanest example of a recurring cost driver in the public data, and H+H already put overtime in its plan. NYPD and DOC are the large ones to watch."
